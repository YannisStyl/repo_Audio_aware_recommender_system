\subsection{Perceptual Listening Experiment}
\label{sec:perceptual_experiment}

To evaluate how human listeners perceive the recommended EQ curves, we conducted a multi-stimulus listening experiment based on the ITU-R BS.1534 (MUSHRA) standard. 

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup and Participant Screening}
We evaluated 22 held-out prompts paired with distinct audio clips across four content categories: Instrumental ($N=7$), Audiobook ($N=8$), Music ($N=4$), and Movie ($N=3$). On each trial, listeners saw a reference audio player labelled \textit{Ref} (playing the original, unprocessed audio) and a text request describing a desired change (e.g., \textit{"Make the mix sound brighter"} or \textit{"Reduce harshness in the vocals"}). Listeners rated seven conditions on a continuous 0--100 scale:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tonmeister 1}: Settings chosen by an expert sound engineer.
    \item \textbf{ICL (Qwen3.5-4B)}: In-context learning baseline using a $5\times$ larger text LLM.
    \item \textbf{Voice-only (0.8B)}: Our model adapted with the speech-focused encoder.
    \item \textbf{FX-only (0.8B)}: Our model adapted with the music and effects encoder.
    \item \textbf{Voice+FX (0.8B Dual)}: Our model combining both audio streams.
    \item \textbf{No Audio (0.8B Baseline)}: The text-only version of the 0.8B model without audio adapters.
    \item \textbf{Hidden Reference}: The unprocessed original audio, hidden among the sliders as an objective control.
\end{enumerate}

The scale labels were explicitly anchored: $0$ (\textit{Opposes the request}), $20$ (\textit{Doesn't address the request}), $40$ (\textit{Slightly addresses}), $60$ (\textit{Moderately addresses}), $80$ (\textit{Mostly addresses}), and $100$ (\textit{Fully addresses}). 

A total of 30 participants completed the experiment. In this setup, the hidden reference slider is identical to the \textit{Ref} button and contains zero modification. Because an unmodified clip cannot achieve the requested change, it represents the semantic baseline ($20$). Participants who gave the unmodified hidden reference an average score above $50$ (\textit{"Moderately addresses the request"}) failed this objective control check, as they repeatedly perceived modifications where none existed. Excluding these 5 listeners leaves $N = 25$ consistent participants ($3,850$ total evaluations, $550$ per condition).

\subsubsection{Perceptual Results}

Table~\ref{tab:perceptual_results} shows the results across all categories. Overall, the human Tonmeister achieved the highest average rating ($43.09 \pm 23.15$, $+13.83$ over raw audio), while the unprocessed reference averaged $29.26 \pm 16.81$, confirming that listeners properly identified the baseline near the $20$--$30$ anchor.

The category breakdown reveals three clear findings:

\textbf{1. Audio Grounding Outperforms Text-Only Baselines In-Domain:}
When evaluated in their intended acoustic settings, the specialized models consistently beat the text-only baseline (\texttt{No Audio}). On Audiobook narration, the \texttt{Voice} model outperforms \texttt{No Audio} by $+5.39$ points ($p = 0.040$, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). On complex Music, the \texttt{FX} model outperforms \texttt{No Audio} by $+6.96$ points ($42.58$ vs. $35.62$). This confirms that physical acoustic features provide grounding that text-only models cannot guess from the prompt alone.

\textbf{2. Specialized 0.8B Models Outperform $5\times$ Larger LLMs:}
On Instrumental tracks, the 0.8B \texttt{Voice} model achieved the highest score in the entire test ($50.42 \pm 25.68$), beating both the text baseline ($42.48, p < 0.001$) and the $5\times$ larger Qwen3.5-4B model ($35.55, p = 2.33 \times 10^{-7}$) by nearly $15$ points. It even outperformed the human Tonmeister ($44.86$). Similarly, on Music, the 0.8B \texttt{FX} model ($42.58$) surpassed Qwen3.5-4B ($39.90$).

\textbf{3. Head-to-Head Win Rates:}
Across the 22 held-out prompts, choosing the best acoustic model for the track ($\max(\text{Voice}, \text{FX})$) beat the text-only baseline on \textbf{17 out of 22 prompts ($77.3\%$ win rate)} and beat Qwen3.5-4B on \textbf{14 out of 22 prompts ($63.6\%$ win rate)}.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we introduced an audio-aware recommender system that uses Q-Former cross-attention adapters and GRPO reinforcement learning to recommend EQ adjustments from natural language instructions. While our specialized models proved highly effective, the results offer two critical lessons regarding decoding strategies and architecture design.

\subsection{Why the Joint Architecture Failed}
The joint dual-stream model (\texttt{Voice+FX}) scored lower than expected ($29.08$), performing on par with the unprocessed reference ($29.26$). A detailed prompt-by-prompt analysis explains this outcome:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High Success on Brightening Requests:} On prompts asking for clarity or brightness (e.g., \textit{"Make the mix sound brighter"} or \textit{"Enhance clarity of the narrator"}), the dual model was often the best-rated model in the entire study, scoring between $60$ and $66$ ($+33$ to $+41$ points above raw audio).
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Failure on Softening Requests:} On prompts asking for warmth, de-essing, or smoothing (e.g., \textit{"Reduce sharpness of 's' sounds"} or \textit{"Add warmth"}), the dual model scored between $12$ and $17$ (well below raw audio). 
    \item \textbf{The Penalty of Active Harm:} Because greedy decoding picked a high-frequency boost mode on these prompts, the model applied the exact opposite of what the listener asked for. On a subjective scale, listeners judge actively making a problem worse far more harshly ($10$--$18$, \textit{"Opposes request"}) than doing nothing at all ($25$--$35$, \textit{"Doesn't address request"}).
    \item \textbf{Multimodal Interference in Small Models:} Injecting 32 continuous audio tokens (16 Voice + 16 FX) into a compact 0.8B backbone saturated its attention capacity, causing the model to lose track of subtle negative text constraints like \textit{"reduce"} or \textit{"smooth"}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Disadvantage of Greedy Decoding for RL Policies}
During GRPO training, our models were optimized with a pairwise diversity reward. This reward explicitly taught the policy to discover multiple valid solution clusters across the coordinate space (e.g., a brightening mode and a softening mode). 

However, for the listening test, we evaluated all models using single greedy decoding ($T=0$). Greedy argmax decoding forces the model to pick only the single most frequent token, collapsing the entire learned distribution into one dominant quadrant. This placed our GRPO-trained policy at a major disadvantage. Reinforcement learning with diversity rewards produces an inherently stochastic, multimodal policy; evaluating it with single greedy selection discards its learned coverage. To realize the full benefits of such models, inference must extract top candidate modes rather than relying on argmax decoding.

\subsection{The Power of Modular Routing}
The clear double-dissociation between our models—where the Voice model dominates speech and solo instruments ($50.42$) while the FX model dominates complex music ($42.58$)—suggests that forcing two disparate audio streams into a single tiny model is unnecessary. 

Instead, a modular system that routes incoming audio to its matching specialist (Voice for speech, FX for music) achieves an overall score of $\mathbf{44.38 \pm 27.63}$ ($+15.12$ over raw audio). This modular approach significantly outperforms both the text-only baseline ($37.92, p = 1.45 \times 10^{-5}$) and the $5\times$ larger Qwen3.5-4B model ($39.22, p = 0.0013$), while matching the quality of a human expert Tonmeister ($43.09$).

\subsection{Conclusion}
Our findings show that acoustic grounding allows compact language models to outperform much larger text-only LLMs on nuanced audio tasks. However, joint multi-stream integration in small models requires careful attention balancing, and diversity-trained policies require mode-aware decoding rather than greedy argmax selection. Modular, stream-specialized architectures provide a robust and practical path forward for intelligent audio recommendation.
